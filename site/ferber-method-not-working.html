Ferber method not working? 10 things to check

By the parent who makes Check In · Updated November 17, 2026

You're several nights in, the crying isn't getting shorter, and you're starting to wonder if this was a mistake. Before you give up, work through this list. Most stalls come down to one or two of these.

The short version. If you're on night 2 or 3, it's probably too early to tell: those nights are often the hardest. After that, the most common reasons are inconsistency (the plan changes from night to night or between parents), falling asleep with help at bedtime, and bedtime timing. If you've been consistent for about two weeks and nothing has changed, talk to your pediatrician. That's Ferber's own limit, and it isn't a failure on your part.1

1It might be working, just not yet

Nights 2 and 3 are often worse than night 1. Your baby is testing whether the old way still works, and that rise in crying is common before things improve. If you're on night 2 or 3, it's too early to judge.

Try: Write down total crying minutes each night and compare the trend over 5 to 7 nights, not one night against the last. The printable chart has space for it, and night by night explains what's typical.

2The plan changes from night to night

Going in early one night, waiting the full time the next, and picking your baby up on the third teaches them that crying longer sometimes works. That makes crying last longer, not shorter. This is the most common reason the method stalls.

Try: Pick waits you can actually stick to, even if they're shorter than the chart, and use them every time. A timer helps more than you'd expect, because at 2am minutes feel much longer than they are.

3Two parents, two different plans

If one parent follows the waits and the other goes in at the first cry, your baby gets mixed messages. It's also the most common thing couples argue about during sleep training.

Try: Agree on the plan in daylight: the waits, what a check-in looks like, and whose turn it is tonight. Taking turns by night, with the other parent out of earshot, helps both of you.

4Your baby still falls asleep with help at bedtime

Ferber's core idea is that a baby who falls asleep being fed, rocked or held at bedtime will want the same thing at every waking. If bedtime still ends with your baby asleep in your arms, the night wakings usually continue.

Try: Move the last feed earlier in the routine (before the bath or the book), and put your baby down drowsy but clearly awake. Bedtime is where the new habit is learned; the night follows.

5The check-ins wind your baby up

Some babies calm down after a check-in. Others get more upset each time a parent appears and leaves. Many parents notice this, and a large survey found that some families switched to fewer or no check-ins along the way.

Try: Keep check-ins short (a minute or two), boring, and in low light, with no picking up. If they still make things worse, use longer waits or skip a check-in. Ferber describes the chart as an example, not a rule.

6Bedtime is too early, or too late

A baby who isn't tired enough will protest for a long time, and so will one who is overtired. Long or late afternoon naps can push bedtime later than you think.

Try: Look at when your baby actually falls asleep on the easier nights, and move bedtime close to that. Ferber's advice is to avoid naps late in the afternoon (after about 4pm) and to start every day at the same time, whatever the night was like.

7The day doesn't start at the same time

Letting your baby sleep in after a rough night feels kind, but it shifts the whole next day and makes the next bedtime harder.

Try: Pick a wake-up time and keep it every morning. Wakings before it are treated as night wakings; at or after it, start the day with lights, curtains and a cheerful good morning.

8Night feeds aren't settled yet

If your baby is genuinely hungry at night, no amount of waiting will help. If they're not, feeding at every waking keeps the wakings going.

Try: Ask your pediatrician whether your baby still needs a night feed. If they do, feed at a planned time and then put them back down awake. Ferber suggests weaning feeds gradually, with smaller amounts and longer gaps, rather than stopping them overnight. More in night wakings and night feeds.

9Your baby is unwell, teething, or in a big change

Colds, ear infections, teething, vaccinations, travel and starting daycare all disrupt sleep. Sleep training during them is harder and can feel unfair.

Try: Comfort your baby when they're unwell. Pause if you need to, and restart when they're better, often by repeating the last night's waits for a night or two before moving on.

10Something in the room keeps waking them

Early light, noise from the rest of the house, or a room that's too warm or too cold can cause wakings that look like a sleep-training problem.

Try: Make the room dark (blackout curtains help with early mornings), keep it quiet or use steady white noise, and dress your baby for the room temperature.

When to stop and talk to your pediatrician

  • You've been consistent for about two weeks and there's no real change.1
  • Your baby seems unwell or in pain: fever, pulling at ears, vomiting, or crying that sounds different from their usual protest.
  • Your baby snores loudly, pauses in breathing, or breathes noisily while asleep.
  • You're worried about feeding or weight, or unsure whether night feeds are still needed.
  • It's taking a real toll on you. Exhaustion and low mood are worth raising too.

Sleep training works for most families who try it: a review of 52 studies found that most reported improvements.3 It isn't the right fit for every baby or every family, and changing approach is a reasonable choice. In one survey, about a quarter of parents ended up using a different method from the one they started with, most often moving to fewer check-ins.2
