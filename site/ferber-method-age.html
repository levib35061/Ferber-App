What age to start the Ferber method

By the parent who makes Check In · Updated November 17, 2026

There's no single right age, but there are some clear signs your baby is ready, and a few things to check with your pediatrician first.

The short version. Most families start sleep training at about 6 months, with a healthy baby who is growing well and whose pediatrician agrees. In a survey of 652 parents, the average age at the start was under 6 months.1 Under about 6 months, talk to your pediatrician first, especially about night feeds. Older babies and toddlers can do it too; the waits are the same, but the practical details change.

Signs your baby may be ready

  • Your baby is healthy and growing well.
  • Your pediatrician agrees they don't need to be fed every time they wake.
  • You have a calm, consistent bedtime routine.
  • You can give it a run of 1 to 2 weeks without travel or big changes.
  • You, and your partner if you have one, are ready to stay consistent.

What to ask your pediatrician

  • Is my baby ready for sleep training?
  • Does my baby still need to feed at night? If so, how often?
  • Is there anything (reflux, ear infections, snoring, weight) that we should sort out first?

Try: take this list to the next check-up. Many pediatricians are happy to talk sleep training at the 6-month visit.

Under 6 months

Young babies still need to eat at night and their sleep is changing quickly, so formal sleep training is usually left until later. What you can do now: a simple, consistent bedtime routine, and putting your baby down drowsy but awake some of the time so falling asleep alone isn't brand new later. Talk to your pediatrician before using timed waits with a younger baby.

6 to 12 months

The most common window. Research trials of graduated check-ins (the Ferber approach) have mostly studied babies in this range, for example a 2016 trial of babies aged 6 to 16 months found better sleep without signs of stress or attachment problems.2 Separation anxiety often peaks somewhere around 8 to 10 months; it can make the first nights louder, but the method is the same.

Toddlers (12 months and up)

It still works, but toddlers bring new problems: they can stand, shout, call for you by name, and eventually climb out. Adjust the practical details, not the principle:

  • Keep check-ins short and boring, exactly as for a baby.
  • A clear, predictable routine matters even more. A picture chart of the routine can help.
  • In a toddler bed, many families use a stair gate at the door so getting out of bed doesn't become a game.
  • Expect more protest at first. Older children have had longer to learn the old way.

When to wait

Hold off if your baby is unwell, teething badly, or about to go through a big change such as travel, a move, a new sibling or starting daycare. Starting two weeks later, with a calm run of nights ahead, beats starting now and stopping on night 3.
