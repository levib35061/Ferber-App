The Ferber method for naps

By the parent who makes Check In · Updated November 17, 2026

Naps are often harder than bedtime: there's less sleep pressure, more daylight, and less time to settle. Here's how to use the same approach in the day without losing your mind.

The short version. Use the same waits as bedtime, but cap each nap attempt: if your baby isn't asleep after about 30 minutes, get them up and try again at the next nap. Ferber also advises against naps late in the afternoon (after about 4pm) so bedtime stays on track.1 Many families start with bedtime only and add naps once nights are going well.

Bedtime first, or naps too?

Both work. Bedtime is usually the easiest place to start because your baby is at their sleepiest, and the skill of falling asleep alone often carries over to naps on its own within a week or two. Doing naps and bedtime together can be quicker but means more crying in a short time.

Try: if you're unsure, start with bedtime. Keep naps however they work now (in arms, in the stroller, in the car) until nights improve, then start naps.

Which waits to use

Use the same row of the chart as the night before. If last night was night 3, today's naps use night 3's waits (10, then 12, then 15 minutes). Keep the same short check-ins: a minute or two, a pat and a few words, no picking up.

Ferber's advice is to keep naps and nights consistent, so your baby gets one message, not two.1

Cap each nap attempt

A nap attempt that turns into an hour of crying wrecks the rest of the day. Ferber suggests ending the attempt if your baby hasn't fallen asleep after about half an hour.1 Many families stretch this to 45 or 60 minutes. Get your baby up, have some calm awake time, and try again at the next nap, or move bedtime a little earlier.

Try: decide your cap before the nap starts, so you're not making it up while your baby is crying.

Short naps

Many babies wake after one sleep cycle, around 30 to 45 minutes. If your baby wakes happy, the nap may simply be over. If they wake crying and clearly still tired, you can treat it like a night waking and use the first wait, but stop after your nap cap and get them up.

Short naps often lengthen on their own once your baby can fall asleep without help, but it can take a few weeks.

Nap timing

A baby who is under-tired protests; an over-tired baby protests too. Watch for your baby's sleepy signs (rubbing eyes, staring off, getting fussy) and keep nap times roughly the same each day. Avoid naps late in the afternoon so bedtime isn't pushed later.1

Babies drop naps as they grow: most go from three naps to two somewhere in the second half of the first year, and from two to one in the second year. A nap that suddenly becomes a battle can mean it's time to drop one.

Daycare and on-the-go naps

You don't need everyone to do it your way. Many babies learn to nap differently in different places. Keep your approach consistent at home, share the basics with caregivers if they're open to it, and don't worry if the occasional stroller or car nap happens.
