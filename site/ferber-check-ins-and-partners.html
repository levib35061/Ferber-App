Ferber check-ins: what to say, how long, and who goes in

By the parent who makes Check In · Updated November 17, 2026

The check-in is the part of the Ferber method that worries parents most. Here's what a good one looks like, and how two parents can share the nights without arguing at 2am.

The short version. Keep it to a minute or two. Low light, calm voice, a pat or a hand on the tummy, the same few words every time, and then leave, even if your baby is still crying. You're there to reassure them, and yourself, "not necessarily to help him stop crying," as Ferber puts it.1 With two parents, decide the plan and whose night it is before bedtime.

A good check-in, step by step

  1. Wait until the timer says it's time. If the crying stops, there's no check-in.
  2. Go in quietly. Keep the lights off or very low.
  3. Stay by the crib. A gentle pat or a hand on their tummy is fine.
  4. Say your few words (below).
  5. Leave while they're still awake, even if they're crying.
  6. Start the next, longer wait as you leave.

What to say

Short, calm and the same every time. For example: "It's sleep time. I'm here. I love you. Goodnight." Repetition is the point: your baby learns the words mean you're close and it's still night.

What to avoid

  • Picking your baby up (unless they're unwell or need a planned feed)
  • Feeding, rocking or holding them until they're asleep
  • Turning on bright lights, or chatting and playing
  • Staying until they stop crying
  • Going in early because the crying sounds worse. Crying usually rises before it falls.

How long to stay

About a minute or two.1 Long enough to reassure, short enough that the visit doesn't become the way your baby falls asleep. A timer for the check-in itself helps, because two minutes in a crying baby's room feels like ten.

If check-ins make it worse

Some babies get more upset each time a parent appears and leaves. If that's your baby, try longer waits or skip some check-ins. Many parents in one large survey ended up using fewer or no check-ins.2 The chart is a guide; consistency matters more than the exact numbers.

Two parents, one plan

The most common sleep-training arguments are about whether a cry is urgent, one parent going in early, and feeling like the other parent doesn't care. They're all easier when the plan is agreed before bedtime, not negotiated at 2am.3

  • Take turns by night. One parent is on duty; the other sleeps, ideally out of earshot or with earplugs.
  • Same script, same waits. Whoever goes in does exactly the same thing.
  • The calmer one goes in. If one of you finds it much harder, let the other take the first nights.
  • Debrief in the morning, not in the middle of the night.

A plan to agree in daylight

  • Bedtime: ___ · Morning wake-up: ___
  • Waits: standard chart, or gentler
  • Our check-in words: "___"
  • Night feed? Yes / no. If yes, when: ___
  • Who's on duty tonight: ___ · Tomorrow: ___
  • We stop and call the pediatrician if: fever, pain, a cry that sounds different, or no change after two weeks.

Try: write it on the back of the printable chart and stick it on the fridge.
