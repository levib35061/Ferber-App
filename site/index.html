The Ferber Method: a practical guide, with the interval chart

Everything you need to do graduated check-ins calmly and consistently: when to start, exactly how long to wait, what to do in the room, and what to expect night by night.

Updated November 16, 2026 · About a 10-minute read · Not medical advice

Quick answer

The Ferber method (also called graduated extinction or progressive waiting) teaches a baby to fall asleep on their own. Put your baby down drowsy but awake, say goodnight and leave. If they cry, wait a set number of minutes, then go in for a brief, calm check-in without picking them up, and leave again. The waits get longer each time and each night, typically starting at 3 minutes on night 1. It's usually started around 6 months, and many families see clear improvement within about a week.

What the Ferber method is

The method comes from pediatrician Dr. Richard Ferber's book Solve Your Child's Sleep Problems (first published in 1985, revised in 2006). The idea is simple: babies who are always rocked, fed or held to sleep often need the same help every time they wake in the night. Learning to fall asleep alone, in their own bed, lets them link sleep cycles without you.

What makes Ferber different from "cry it out" is the check-ins. You don't leave your baby alone all night. You come back at planned, gradually longer intervals to reassure them that you're nearby, and leave before they fall asleep, so the falling asleep part is theirs.

Is your baby ready?

  • Age: sleep training is usually recommended from around 6 months. Younger babies still need more night-time help and feeds.
  • Health: your baby is healthy and growing well, and isn't currently sick.
  • Feeds: ask your pediatrician whether your baby still needs a night feed. If so, you can keep it on a plan and still use check-ins for other wakings.
  • Your week: pick a stretch of about a week with no travel, visitors or big changes, when you can be consistent.
Talk to your pediatrician first. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Your pediatrician knows your baby.

Before your first night

  • A short, predictable bedtime routine (for example: bath, pajamas, feed, book, song, bed) at the same time each night.
  • Move the feed earlier in the routine so your baby isn't fed all the way to sleep.
  • A safe sleep space: on their back, in a crib with a firm, flat mattress and nothing else in it.
  • Agree on the plan with your partner, including who does check-ins and when. Inconsistency is the most common reason it stalls.
  • Choose your check-in words, something short like "It's sleep time, I love you, goodnight."
  • Have a timer ready. Minutes feel like hours at night, and guessing usually means going in early.

Step by step

  1. Do your bedtime routine and put your baby in the crib drowsy but awake.
  2. Say your goodnight phrase and leave the room.
  3. If your baby cries, start the timer for the first wait of tonight (see the chart). If they're just babbling or fussing quietly, there's no need to go in.
  4. When the wait is up, check in for a minute or two: calm voice, a brief pat, your phrase. Don't pick them up.
  5. Leave while they're still awake, even if they're still crying, and start the next, longer wait.
  6. Repeat until they fall asleep. After the last wait in the chart, keep repeating that same wait.
  7. If they wake later in the night, start again from the first wait for that night.
  8. Next night, move to the next row of the chart. Keep going until your baby falls asleep on their own.

Example: night 2

Your baby starts crying at 7:40. You wait 5 minutes, check in, and leave at 7:46. They keep crying, so you wait 10 minutes, check in, and leave at 7:58. From then on you wait 12 minutes each time until they're asleep. If they wake at 2:00, you start again at 5 minutes.

What to do during a check-in

  • Keep it short: about 1 to 2 minutes.
  • Keep it calm and boring: lights low, quiet voice, a brief pat on the back or tummy.
  • Say the same phrase every time.
  • Don't pick them up, feed, or rock them to sleep. If they're standing, you can lay them down once; after that, it's fine to leave them standing.
  • Leave while they're still awake, and start the next wait when you walk out.

The check-in isn't meant to stop the crying. It's there to show your baby you haven't disappeared, and to let you see that they're safe.

Night wakings and naps

Night wakings

Handle a waking the same way as bedtime: wait the first interval for that night, check in, and keep going through the chart. If your pediatrician has advised keeping a night feed, give it as planned, then put your baby back down awake.

Naps

You can use the same waits for naps. Many families cap a nap attempt at about 30 to 60 minutes: if your baby hasn't fallen asleep by then, get them up and try again at the next nap. Some families start with bedtime only and add naps once nights improve.

Early-morning wakings

Pick a reasonable morning wake-up time. Wakings before it are treated as night wakings; at or after it, start the day with a clear "good morning" so your baby learns the difference.

How long it takes

Many families see clear improvement within about a week, though every baby is different. A typical pattern:

  • Night 1: often the longest crying, as everything is new.
  • Nights 2 to 3: crying can get worse before it gets better. This is common and usually temporary; it's a sign the old habit is being tested, not that it's failing.
  • Nights 4 to 7: crying usually shortens and check-ins become rarer.

Tracking crying minutes each night helps a lot. On a hard night it's reassuring to see the trend going down.

Common problems

Stuck after several nights? See Ferber method not working: 10 things to check.

Crying got worse on night 2 or 3

This is common. Stay consistent with the chart; changing approach now often makes the process longer. If you need to, repeat the previous night's waits rather than going back to rocking or feeding to sleep.

My baby vomits when crying hard

Go in, clean up calmly and quickly with low light, then continue where you were. If it happens often, talk to your pediatrician.

My baby is sick, teething, or we're traveling

Pause and comfort your baby as they need. Pick the plan back up when things are back to normal, usually by repeating an earlier night's waits.

My partner and I can't agree

Agree on the whole plan before night 1, including who goes in. It helps to have one person doing check-ins on a given night, and to use a shared timer so neither of you is guessing.

A sibling shares the room

Some families move the older child for the first few nights, or start with naps when the sibling is out of the room.

It's been two weeks and nothing has changed

Check for consistency, an early-enough bedtime, and whether a feed or nap is out of place. If you're still stuck, talk to your pediatrician.

What the research says

Many parents worry that leaving a baby to cry, even with check-ins, could cause harm. Studies so far haven't found long-term harm from behavioral sleep techniques like graduated check-ins:

  • A randomized trial in Australia followed children whose families used behavioral sleep techniques in infancy to age 6. It found no differences in emotional or behavioral problems, child stress, or the parent-child relationship compared with families who didn't (Price and colleagues, Pediatrics, 2012).
  • A 2016 randomized trial compared graduated extinction and bedtime fading with a control group. Both improved infant sleep, with no signs of increased stress or attachment problems at follow-up (Gradisar and colleagues, Pediatrics, 2016).

Sleep training isn't required, and it isn't right for every family. If it feels wrong, gentler approaches are fine too.
